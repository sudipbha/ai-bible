Chapter 2 — The Worker's Failure Map

Chapter 1 left you with two questions (can you check the work at a glance, and could you survive the worst version?) and the rule they add up to: delegate only what passes both. That rule works before you delegate. This chapter is about what happens after: the specific ways the work comes back wrong. Because here is the useful secret about the strangest employee you'll ever hire: its failures are not random. They cluster. They have shapes. And once you can name the shapes, you can predict where a task will break before you hand it over, the way you know without thinking which jobs the new hire will fumble and which they can't hurt.

One warning before the map. A person who is about to fail usually shows it: the pause, the hedge, the "let me get back to you." This worker shows nothing. Every failure below arrives in the same fluent, organized, confident voice as its best work. The map exists because the voice tells you nothing.

The five ways it fails

1. It fills gaps with inventions. When the machine doesn't know something, it does not say "I don't know" and stop. It produces something plausible that fits the shape of an answer. Ask for court cases and it will give you court cases: correctly formatted, persuasively named, and sometimes nonexistent. Amir Mostafavi, a California employment lawyer running his own practice, filed an appeals brief in which 21 of 23 quotations turned out to be fabricated by the AI tools he'd used. The court fined him $10,000, reported at the time as the largest such fine in the state, and published its opinion as a warning to the profession (Noland v. Land of the Free, September 2025; court record). Note what his story adds to Karlen's from Chapter 1: Karlen was a business owner who couldn't check legal work. Mostafavi is a lawyer. The gap-filling failure got them both, because catching an invented citation requires checking every citation, and the polish gives you no reason to.

The tell that isn't there: invented material reads more confident than real material, not less. The machine is at its most specific exactly where it has the least.

2. It drops things silently. Ask it to summarize, merge, or clean, and it will usually get most of it right and quietly lose a piece. This book ran its own logged trials: six owner-sized tasks, each run past two AI workers, a cheap one and an expensive one, every output checked against the source. The cheap worker's supplier-terms summary dropped one payment condition; its contact-list cleanup merged two duplicates and silently lost a note attached to one of them. No error message. No "I skipped something." The output looks complete because completeness is a look. A person who loses a piece of your paperwork usually knows they lost it. This worker does not know, which is why the check for this family is always a count: count the rows, re-add the total, tick off the clauses against the original.

3. It misreads with authority. The most dangerous family, and the one Chapter 1 made you feel: given a lease clause, the cheap worker explained, fluently and helpfully, that staying on would hand the tenant the exact tenancy arrangement the clause exists to deny. Everything around the error was right, which is what made the error invisible. Misreadings don't look like mistakes; they look like explanations. They cluster where the source material is technical and the reader can't independently judge it: contracts, regulations, tax rules, medical guidance. New York City ran a version of this experiment at city scale: its official small-business help chatbot told owners they could take a cut of workers' tips and refuse cash, both illegal (The Markup, March 2024). Official deployment, government branding, confidently wrong about the law it existed to explain. The rule this family teaches: verify against the source of the rule, never against the bot.

4. It leaks its template. The machine has never met your business. When it needs a fact it doesn't have — your company name, your prices, your policy — it will sometimes leave the socket showing: our trials' cheap worker sent back a customer-ready email with "[Your Company Name]" still sitting in it. This is the least dangerous family because it's the most visible, and it is the reminder the others need: the worker is assembling patterns, not remembering you. Which leads to the family that owners find hardest to believe.

5. It doesn't stay corrected. Correct a person Tuesday and they arrive corrected Thursday. Chapter 1 told you why the machine may not: memory features exist, but no vendor claims them as reliable learning from your corrections, and the vendors' own documentation warns the models make things up. So treat every correction as a correction to the draft, never to the worker. The practical consequence is a habit: anything you find yourself correcting twice goes into your written instructions, the standing prompt, the script, the facts sheet you paste in, because the paper remembers what the worker won't. And if more than one of you types into these tools, the paper must be shared: one facts sheet where everyone can reach it, one named owner who updates it when a correction repeats, or shape 5 just moves from the machine's memory into each person's separate chat history.

After this chapter, you can:

  • Name the five failure shapes and predict which one a given task invites
  • Match each of your delegated tasks to the check that catches its shape
  • Explain why "are you sure?" and AI-checking-AI are not verification
  • Write the first rows of your own failure map, with a take-back line

The map: task families and the checks that fit them

The reason Chapter 1's named checks work is that each one is aimed at a failure shape. Here is the whole instrument on one page, built from the six task families in this book's logged trials and the shapes above:

Task familyMost likely failureThe check that catches itIf the check fails you
Drafts you'll send (one-off replies, emails)template leakage; tone offread it once before it sendsworst case is an apology
Anything with arithmetic (quotes, invoices)silent omission or slipre-add the total; tick lines against your standard list for the jobwrong price to one customer
Summaries of documents you holdsilent omissiontick each item against the originala missed condition surfaces later
List and data cleanupsilent loss in mergescount rows; re-read merged entriesa lost note, a lost customer
Answers from your own facts sheetinvention past the sheet's edgecheck against the sheet; forbid guessinga wrong promise in writing
Interpreting rules you can't judge (legal, tax, medical)authoritative misreadingnone you can runa confident wrong answer you can't detect, which is why it isn't delegated

One scope line on the first row: it means the ordinary one-off reply. The moment a draft states a price, a promise, or a policy, or goes out in bulk, its worst case stops being an apology, and it takes the facts-sheet row's discipline instead: source attached, numbers recomputed, no guessing past the sheet.

Two things about that last row. First, it is not a gap in you; it is the definition of the family (Chapter 1's take-it-back verdict, now with its mechanism attached). Second, the machine still earns a seat beside those tasks: drafting the questions for your lawyer, organizing the documents for your accountant, translating your situation into a clear brief. That is labor pointed at the survivable half of the job.

Why you can't outsource the checking to the machine

Every owner has the same idea within a week: "I'll just ask it to double-check its own work." Two reasons that fails. The first is mechanical: asking "are you sure?" doesn't consult anything new. The machine generates a fresh answer in the same fluent voice, and it can just as readily apologize and "correct" a right answer into a wrong one — the confidence doesn't budge either way (a mechanism claim from how these systems work, not a measured rate; our own trials didn't test the are-you-sure flip). The second reason has a court record. Brandon Monk, a Texas attorney, filed AI-generated citations and then used a second AI legal tool to check them. The checker missed the fabrications. He was fined $2,000 and ordered into AI education (Gauthier v. Goodyear, November 2024; sanction and order per the court record, the second-tool detail per the case's reporting). One machine's confident guess does not become verification because a second machine agreed; the lesson of his case is the book's rule: checks run against reality (your documents, your numbers, the source of the rule) or they are not checks.

And notice who this book's lawyer stories are about: small firms and solo practices, chosen because they are your scale, not because big firms are immune. The record repeats at that scale. Chong Ke, at a small Vancouver family-law firm, was ordered to personally pay for the time wasted on two invented cases she had filed (CBC, February 2024; the first reported Canadian instance). And one honest note about the pile: its one business owner is Chapter 1's Jonathan Karlen; nearly every other name is a lawyer, not because lawyers are careless, but because courts publish their failures. Your trade's version doesn't make the paper; it just makes a bad week. That is why this book ran its own logged trials on owner-sized tasks instead of waiting for your case file. Mostafavi, Monk, and Ke are not stupid people. They are busy people in checkable-looking situations where the checking was the whole job, and the polish said it was already done. Your week contains the same trap at smaller stakes, which is the only reason this chapter exists.

What this means for the leash

Chapter 1 gave you three firing rules (its two questions screened what to hand over; the firing rules govern taking work back). The map refines the leash they tug:

  • Shape 1 tasks (invention risk) get delegated only with a source attached: the facts sheet, the price list, the policy document, plus the standing instruction if it isn't in the source, say so instead of answering. That instruction does not make inventions impossible. It gives them fewer places to hide: an answer fenced to your sheet is an answer you can check against your sheet. Your check does the rest.
  • Shape 2 and 4 tasks (omission and leakage) are the sweet spot: cheap to check by count and glance, survivable when the check slips. This is where the labor earns its keep, and where most of your delegations should live for the first months. One fence before the first paste: a customer list is customer data. Strip what identifies people before it goes into a consumer tool, because consumer tiers train on conversations by default (Chapter 12 carries the switches and the full fence).
  • Shape 3 tasks (misreading) stay behind the line from Chapter 1. The map adds the why: the check for a misreading requires the expertise the delegation was meant to replace. There is no discipline that fixes a circular check. There is only not delegating it.
  • Shape 5 (no staying power) is not a task family but a property of the worker, and it sets your management style: instructions live in documents, not in the worker's memory of being told.

One honest boundary on the map itself. It is built from this book's twelve logged runs, the public court record, and the vendors' own documentation: demonstrations and cases, not statistics. Capability moves; the shapes have been stable because they follow from how the machine works, not from this month's model. When a vendor claims a shape is fixed, the map's answer is the same as for any worker with a new reference letter: run your checks a while longer and let the record tell you.

Do this Friday

About thirty minutes, $0.

  1. List your five most repeated tasks from the last two weeks. Actual tasks, not categories: "reply to the Tuesday review," "retype the supplier order into the sheet."
  2. Give each a row: which task family above; which failure shape it invites; which check catches that shape; what the worst survivable version costs. (One worked as a model: "reply to the Tuesday review" is a draft you'll send; it invites template leakage and tone slips; the check is reading it before it sends; the worst survivable version costs an apology.) A task can invite more than one shape, and then it gets one row per shape: a quote email is a draft you'll send and arithmetic, a tone row and a price row, and you run both checks.
  3. Mark the take-backs. Anything landing in the last row, or anything whose check you honestly won't run on a busy Thursday, gets marked not delegated, on paper, so future-you doesn't relitigate it at 9 p.m.
  4. Attach one source. For your best delegation candidate, assemble its facts sheet once: prices, policies, the three answers you always give (keep customer-identifying details out of it on consumer tools; their consumer tiers train on conversations by default, and Chapter 12 carries the switches and the full fence). Ten minutes now; it gives shape 1 fewer places to hide on every future run of that task.

Your map will have five tasks, and a few more rows where they split. The book's has six families. Growth is adding rows, not trust.

The takeaway: the machine's failures have shapes — invention, silent loss, authoritative misreading, template leakage, and no memory of your corrections — and a delegation is safe exactly when its shape has a check you'll actually run.

Chapter 3 — The Five-Question Filter

DoNotPay sold itself as "the world's first robot lawyer." For a subscription, it promised to fight parking tickets, generate legal documents, and let you sue anyone at the press of a button. No law degree, no billable hours, no retainer. For an owner with a dispute worth less than a lawyer's first hour, the pitch lands exactly where it hurts.

In September 2024, the Federal Trade Commission announced that DoNotPay had agreed to pay $193,000 and to stop claiming its AI matched a human lawyer unless it could prove it. The FTC's complaint was almost boring. The company had not tested whether its robot lawyer worked at the level of a human attorney. It had not even hired lawyers to check. The final order, approved unanimously in January 2025, added one requirement more painful than the fine: DoNotPay had to tell its own past subscribers about the settlement.

Notice what the FTC did not say. It did not say AI cannot help with legal paperwork. It said nobody had checked whether this AI could. The product had never been reviewed. The marketing, eventually, was.

That enforcement action made DoNotPay one of the most scrutinized AI products in America. The tens of thousands of other tools on the market have faced less. There is no Consumer Reports for AI, and the regulator shows up occasionally, after the damage. Nobody is coming to review the tool you are about to buy.

So the review has to be yours, and short enough that you will actually run it. This chapter is that review: five questions, one index card, about thirty minutes. The filter is distilled from three sources that take the problem seriously: the Small Business Administration's vendor checklists, the AI Risk Management Framework from NIST (the federal standards body), and MIT's research on why AI pilots fail. You get the five questions that survive translation into plain English.

Think of this as the job interview for Chapter 1's strangest employee, and start with the question that decides whether a candidate even gets the interview.

Question zero: who profits from me believing this?

Every claim that reaches you arrived on someone's payroll. The vendor profits if you believe the demo. The affiliate reviewer profits if you click their "honest comparison" (Chapter 5 breaks down the commission rates). The course seller profits if you feel behind. None of that makes a claim false, but it sets the burden of proof: a paid voice earns belief with evidence, not enthusiasm. Ask question zero out loud, name who profits, and most of the noise disqualifies itself before the interview starts. Whatever survives gets the five questions. Expect most to fail by question three. That is the filter working.

After this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Put any AI tool through five questions on an index card in about thirty minutes
  • Price a tool at your real volume (seats, tasks, add-ons, twelve months) instead of its sticker price
  • Spot where a "free" plan actually ends before it traps a working habit
  • Check a vendor's data practices in ten minutes on their own site, and find the exit before you buy
  • Run a 30-day pilot with one metric and written kill criteria, so tools stay or go on evidence

Question 1: What task, and measured how?

A tool that promises to "transform your marketing" is making you do its thinking. Which task? "Send the weekly special to the email list every Thursday" is a task. "Answer the phone when we're closed" is a task. "Marketing" is a department.

Then walk the chain to a number, link by link. The tool drafts the Thursday email. The email goes out on time instead of skipping busy weeks. More customers open it. Some order. If every link holds, the tool earns money. If you cannot walk the chain, the benefit is a slogan. MIT's much-quoted pilot research points the same way if you look only at the tools that survived. The kept tools produced a measurable result inside a real workflow, inside a defined window. The merely impressive got dropped. That reading is this book's, not the study's own claim, and the study watched big companies, not shops; Chapter 10 sizes it honestly.

Before any trial, write down the current number. How long does Thursday's email take today? How many calls went unanswered last week? A baseline costs five minutes now and settles every argument later.

Owners who win with AI talk in numbers. When a Florida news outlet profiled Growbo, a small marketing agency, founder Matt Ackerson could put figures on his AI systems: about 700 hours and $12,000 saved in four months. The figures are his own and the article shows no ledger behind them, so add salt. The point survives the salt: he had numbers because he measured, which is the whole ask of question one.

The rule: a tool you cannot measure is a tool you cannot fire. It will sit in the subscription graveyard drawing $29 a month forever, protected by the vague feeling that it helps, until Chapter 13's audit finds it.

Question 2: Who runs it every week?

The most common review of small-business software is eight words long: "We should really be using that thing more."

Software does not run itself, and AI software especially does not. Someone connects it to the calendar and the inbox. Someone feeds it this week's prices. Someone reads what it wrote before a customer does. In Bluevine's 2026 survey, 78% of small-business owners said they do not fully trust AI to work unsupervised. They are right; Chapter 2 showed the machine lying with perfect confidence. Supervision is not a flaw in the plan. It is the plan.

So name the supervisor before you buy. A name and an hour: "Nadia, Friday, 8 to 9." If the answer is "the team," the answer is nobody. If the answer is you (and in a business your size it usually is), question two has a second half: which hour of your week does this replace? A tool that needs an hour of babysitting must save clearly more than that hour, or it is a hobby with an invoice.

Question 3: Where does my data go?

Every AI tool is a new place where your business information lives. What goes in is rarely just "text": it is customer names and phone numbers, your prices and margins, the draft policy about the employee problem you asked for help wording.

It is probably already happening informally. MIT researchers reported in 2025 that at more than 90% of the companies they studied (big companies again, not shops), employees were using personal AI accounts at work, pasting work material into free consumer chatbots nobody vetted. Owners have noticed the stakes: 33% named data security as a barrier in Bluevine's 2026 survey, up from 23% the year before. The worry is growing because usage is growing.

You do not need a security department. You need three answers, findable on the vendor's own site in ten minutes:

  1. Does it train on my data, and is there an off switch? Business plans usually say no or let you opt out; free consumer tiers often say yes, quietly.
  2. Can I export everything, and delete everything, if I leave?
  3. Which plan is built for business use? The free version and the business version of the same product can carry different privacy terms.

Until you have checked, treat a free AI tool like a postcard: write nothing on it you would mind someone else reading. Customer data belongs on a business plan with the training switch off. That includes the trial: if the candidate task touches live customer data from day one (the phone tool will), pilot on the business tier and count its cost in the trial budget, or run the first week on a test line and dummy records before anything real connects. If the tool records or transcribes calls or meetings, one more rule no vendor page will surface: your state or province may require the other person's consent before recording; check that rule before the pilot, not after. And if you handle health, financial, or children's information, these three answers are necessary, not sufficient: ask a professional whether you need a signed data agreement (in US healthcare, a business associate agreement) before that data touches any AI tool. Chapter 12 goes deeper, including the tribunal case that made an airline honor a discount its chatbot had described wrong (one small ruling; the principle travels): what your tools promise, you keep.

Question 4: What does it cost at real volume?

The sticker price is the price of the demo. The real price arrives around month six, and it is set by how software charges: per seat, per task, per "operation," per employee, per resolved ticket.

An example with honest math. Intercom's AI support agent runs $29 per seat per month, plus 99 cents for every conversation the AI resolves (August 2026 snapshot; check the vendor's page). Resolve 300 tickets a month — a good month — and the bill is roughly $326, not $29. The tool may still be worth it. But that decision belongs to the $326 number.

Free tiers deserve the same math, because they are sized to end just as the habit forms. That is not an accident; it is the business model. Here is where "free" actually stops, as of August 2026. Prices change, so check the vendor's page before you buy.

Question 5: How do I get out?

A tracker called Killed by AI collects dead AI products the way other sites collect obituaries (Chapter 14 teaches the warning signs of a dying tool). The count matters less than the pattern: AI tools are young companies, and young companies fold, pivot, and get acquired. Some of your candidates will not exist in three years.

Nobody has published a reliable count of small businesses trapped in AI tools they regret; the lock-in data does not exist yet. The mechanics are visible anyway. Your data accumulates inside the tool. Your workflows grow around it. Then the price rises, or a free feature moves behind a paywall, precisely because leaving now hurts. The vendor is not evil. The vendor has an incentive, and you should be able to name it.

Four checks before the first dollar:

  1. Find the export button before you need it. No export is your answer.
  2. Check the format. Spreadsheet files you can open anywhere are an exit; a backup only the same tool can read is a wall.
  3. Go month-to-month for the first year, even when the annual plan dangles two free months. The discount is the price of your flexibility. No month-to-month tier exists at all (the table's Otter row is one)? Then the annual lock-in is itself an exit cost: weigh it as one, and move your kill review to day 60 so the decision doesn't wait for a renewal date.
  4. Write down what breaks on cancel day. Which forms, calendars, phone lines, and customer messages run through this tool? That list is your real exit cost.

Industry-specific platforms — the restaurant systems, the trades apps — lock deepest, because they hold the most. Sometimes they are still the right call. Chapter 9 weighs that trade honestly.

The 30-day pilot

A tool that passes question zero and all five questions has earned a trial. Not a purchase; a trial with a scoreboard. (Chapter 10 expands this trial into the full probation practice, with the card and the weekly review.)

The famous failure statistic makes the case. In 2025, MIT researchers found that 95% of formal corporate AI pilots showed no measurable return six months later. That study covered big companies, not businesses like yours, and its bar was strict. But the reason those pilots failed is portable: they launched without a metric, an owner, or a deadline, then drifted. Buy-and-drift has a small-business version too. In a 2025 NEXT Insurance survey (n=1,500), the share of small businesses using AI fell from 42% to 28% in a year; among non-users, 62% said they did not understand the benefits, and 55% pointed at cost. The survey doesn't say how many of the missing bought and quit versus never started; either way, nothing they could count kept them.

The pilot avoids both endings. Four rules:

  1. Thirty days, on the calendar. Renewal is a decision, not a default.
  2. One metric, chosen from question one, with the baseline written down first.
  3. Kill criteria in writing before day one. "If it has not saved two hours a week by day 30, it goes." Deciding while neutral beats deciding while attached.
  4. One pilot at a time. Two simultaneous trials means neither gets a fair test.

Here is the quiet advantage you have over the companies in that MIT study: their pilots need a committee, a budget line, and a quarter. You can baseline a task this afternoon and know in thirty days, usually for under a hundred dollars (your question-four number is the real figure; a per-resolution tool like the Intercom example can pass $300 in a good month). Run the pilot like an owner and the market's failure rate is not your problem.

A pilot is not a vibe check. It is a bet with a scoreboard, and either outcome — stay or go — is a win, because both end the ambiguity that fills subscription graveyards.

Do This Friday

Time required: about 45 minutes: the same thirty the filter takes, plus fifteen to price the truth and schedule the pilot. Cost: $0.

  1. Pick one candidate. The tool you are most tempted by right now, or one you're unsure enough about that Chapter 13's audit would file it under "Prove." Just one.
  2. Write the five answers on one index card. The task and its number. The owner and their hour. The three data answers from the vendor's site. The real-volume annual cost. The exit.
  3. Price the truth (the deep version of the card's cost answer). Real monthly volume × unit price, plus seats, times twelve. Put that number next to the sticker price.
  4. Find the exit before the entrance (the card's exit answer, verified). Locate the export feature, check the file format, read the cancellation terms, note the contract length.
  5. Schedule the pilot. Start date, baseline number, one metric, a 30-day review in your calendar, and a written kill criterion.
  6. Let a blank mean no. Any question you cannot answer is a "not yet." Saying no to software is free, and you can say yes later. (Appendix B has this filter as a printable worksheet.)

The takeaway: if a tool cannot survive five questions on an index card and a 30-day pilot with a kill date, it does not deserve a line on your credit-card statement.

Chapter 4 — Never Miss Another Customer

On a Sunday morning last year, a plumbing company booked a $1,060 job while the owner was still in bed.

Nobody at Riley Plumbing & Heating answered that call, because nobody was working. An AI phone agent picked up, talked the customer through the problem, checked the schedule, and booked the visit. That story comes from ServiceTitan, the company that sells the tool, so take the shine off it accordingly. But the mechanics are not marketing. The call came in. Something answered. The job got booked. At most small businesses, that same call rings four times, goes to voicemail, and dies there.

This chapter is about some of the cheapest revenue you will ever find: the customers already trying to give you money.

Run Chapter 1's two questions on this domain and you'll see why it goes first. Can you check the machine's phone work at a glance? Yes, faster than any other delegation: every booking lands in your calendar and every call leaves a log, so the checks are your own records. Could you survive the worst version? With a narrow script and a staffed human handoff, yes: a fumbled booking is an apology and a callback. The phone is floor-work (the visible, low-stakes, everyday tasks) with the receipts built in, which is exactly the profile Chapter 2's failure map says to delegate first.

  • Measure your missed-call leak in dollars per week from your own phone log

The leak you're not measuring

Here is the uncomfortable math, with its sourcing shown, because these numbers are shakier than the phone-answering industry admits. The only actual measurement we found is a vendor's own: a local-marketing firm monitored the phone lines of 85 small businesses for a month and clocked roughly 62% of calls going unanswered. Nearly four in ten reached voicemail, and a quarter rang into nothing (411 Locals' self-published study, January 2016; a decade old, and the exact split is in this chapter's source note). Small sample, seller's pen. Treat it as directional. And the companion statistic every vendor repeats, that 85% of callers who reach voicemail never call back, traces to no study at all. We followed the citations; they run in a circle of vendors quoting each other. What survives without any study is the part you know from your own dialing habits: people don't leave a message. They call the next name on the list. You never know they existed.

Put real numbers on it. Say your average job is worth $300, and you close half the people you actually talk to. Every ten missed calls is ten conversations you never had, five jobs you never won, $1,500 that went to a competitor whose phone got answered. That is the ceiling, not the count: subtract the spam, the repeat callers, the ones who tried again, and the current customers who would have reached you anyway. Friday's count of your own log settles it; no one else's multiplier is required. Run that math across a year and you understand why industry estimates of the annual cost of missed calls for a small service business run from $26,000 to $126,000 depending on the trade. That range is vendor math, so treat it as directional; the per-call arithmetic above, done on your own log, is the number that counts. (If your work is retainer clients rather than inbound quote calls, your leak lives in Chapter 6's quiet threads, not the ring count.)

Meanwhile, most owners trying to "do AI" are generating social media posts. Marketing pours water into the top of a leaky bucket. This chapter plugs the hole in the bottom.

The order matters: capture the demand you already have before you spend a dollar creating more.

Why speed is the whole game

Sales research has repeated one finding for years, and its actual source is older and humbler than the legend: in a 2007 InsideSales.com report (the company sells lead-response software, so it has a stake in the finding) coauthored by then-MIT Sloan faculty fellow James Oldroyd, an observational analysis of more than 15,000 web leads across six companies associated a first call at five minutes with about 21 times the odds of reaching the companies' qualification threshold compared with a first call at 30 minutes; qualification meant entering the sales process, not completing a sale. Nobody comparison-shops patiently anymore. A homeowner with a burst pipe calls until a human, or something that sounds helpfully close to one, picks up.

You cannot personally be that fast at 7 p.m. on a Tuesday. You are quoting a job, or making dinner, or finally off the clock. For decades the options were an answering service, a hired dispatcher, or your own interrupted evening. The genuinely new thing AI brings is a fourth option: software that answers every call, instantly, for about the price of one small job a month.

Owners who set this up describe the same two results. First, booked work they would have missed. Second, and they mention this almost as often: the phone stops owning them. Dinner is dinner. That second result is why this book keeps insisting AI's best gift to a small business is not head count math. It is getting pieces of your life back: capacity and relief, never a promise about cutting people.

What "answering" needs to include

An AI receptionist that just takes messages is voicemail with better manners. The setups that make money do four things in a row. Think of it as one pipeline: answer → book → follow up → ask for the review.

  1. Answer every call, 24/7. The AI greets callers, handles the three questions that make up most of your volume (are you open, do you serve my area, what does it roughly cost), and takes details for anything unusual.
  2. Book straight into your calendar. Not "someone will call you back." The caller hangs up holding a time slot. G.L. Hunt, a family-owned Texas foundation-repair company, reports a 40% higher booking rate after wiring AI follow-up into its missed-call handling. That number comes from Hatch, its software vendor, so treat it as a best case. The math that matters is smaller and yours: the AI answering tools below run $79–99 a month, so the break-even is one question. Does your log show at least one bookable call a month slipping away, worth more to you than the fee? That is the whole business case.
  3. Follow up automatically. Missed a call anyway? The system texts back within a minute: "Sorry we missed you — want to book a time or tell us what you need?" A text that arrives while the customer is still on your competitor's hold music wins ugly and often. One law check before the texting goes on: automated texts are regulated (TCPA in the US, CASL in Canada), and a single text back to a number that just called you sits differently under those rules than an ongoing message stream. Make sure the tool includes an opt-out line, and confirm its consent posture against the source of the rule, never the vendor's FAQ (Chapter 12).
  4. Ask for the review. Job done, the system requests a Google review and drafts your reply. Reviews feed the next customer's decision, which feeds the phone, which is the whole loop. Two rules keep the loop legal and welcome: the ask goes to everyone or no one (tools that quietly screen out unhappy customers first are doing review gating, which Google's rules prohibit and US regulators have moved against), and the ask waits until the job is actually settled, no open complaint or callback, with repeat customers asked at most a few times a year.

Restaurants run the same play with orders instead of appointments. Little Italy, an independent restaurant, put an AI agent on its phone line connected to its Toast register and went live in three days (the vendor, Loman, tells the story, same caveat as above). The vendor's telling leads with a front-of-house labor-cost cut above 24%, which is the exact pitch this book promised not to make. So read that number as proof the phones got covered, not as a payroll plan. Three stories, three vendors' pens, each labeled as it passed: that is the honest state of the public evidence at this scale. And one decoupling before the shopping list: those stories belong to ServiceTitan, Hatch, and Loman, which are not the tools in the table below. The stories show what the category can do; the tools below have prices and trial windows, not published proof, and your 30-day log is what turns a trial into evidence. The one number in this chapter you can trust outright is the one you pull from your own call log on Friday.

What stays human, and the check that keeps it honest

Two call classes get taken back before any tool goes live, in writing, in the script. Complaints and upset customers: the machine takes a message and flags it urgent; a person calls back. The setup names that person: whose phone the urgent flag lands on, and the longest a flag may wait before a second person picks it up. An angry customer handled by a bot is an escalation you paid to create (Chapter 9 documents a customer stuck in a bot loop across two days over a $14 salad; that is the failure mode, and it is optional). Anything with legal, medical, or emergency weight: whatever your trade's version is (the gas-smell call, the flooded-basement call, the reaction-to-treatment call), those route to a human, always, and the script says so. For true emergencies the script also says the direct thing first: call 911 or your utility's emergency line now, not a callback. And one standing ritual keeps the whole delegation honest: once a week, listen to or read five of the machine's calls against your script (every tool worth buying keeps logs). Twenty minutes; it is the difference between supervising this worker and hoping about it.

Three warnings before you switch anything on

Your AI's promises are your promises. In 2024 a Canadian tribunal made Air Canada honor a bereavement discount its chatbot had described wrong: the airline's real policy required asking before the flight, and the book-now-claim-later version the bot offered didn't exist. The ruling was small money and a giant warning: the company owns what the bot says. Keep your AI's script narrow. It should answer the questions you gave it, book the appointment, and say "I'll have someone confirm that" for everything else. Never let it improvise prices, guarantees, or availability you didn't write down. Chapter 12 goes deeper.

Say it's an AI up front, check the rules, and staff the handoff. Have the bot introduce itself as an assistant instead of waiting to be asked: some places require upfront identification by law for bots that book or sell, recorded or transcribed calls carry their own notice-and-consent duties, and ten minutes with your province's or state's rules before launch (the source of the rule, per Chapter 12, never the vendor's FAQ) is part of the setup, not optional polish. Some callers will want a human: a message, an urgent flag, and a person who actually sees it. An AI receptionist with no human backstop is how you miss the one call that mattered while feeling covered.

Know where the recordings live. A phone AI records and transcribes strangers, and callers blurt things they would only tell you: the panicked tax question, the address, the medical detail. Before switch-on, read the tool's data page (where calls and transcripts are stored, for how long, whether they train models). Chapter 12's fence (where your data may and may not go) applies to the phone line too.

Do this Friday

Time required: about an hour. Cost: $0 today.

  1. Count the leak. Open your phone's call log for the last seven days. Count calls you missed entirely and calls returned more than an hour later. Multiply by your close rate and average job value. Write that number down. That's your leak, per week.
  2. Call your own business after closing time, from a friend's phone. Listen to what a new customer hears. Most owners find this genuinely painful. Good. Use the pain.
  3. Script your top three. Write the three questions you answer most, with your real answers, in plain words. Add the booking rule: the machine may hand out a firm slot only for the job types you list as safe to book unseen; everything else gets a callback window, not a slot. Set your travel buffer and service zone in the calendar before the first live call. Whatever tool you pick, this script is its training material, and writing it costs nothing.
  4. Pick one tool and book the trial. One, not three. If you take calls for a living, start with Goodcall or My AI Front Desk. Appointment-based? Start with Calendly plus an AI receptionist that writes into it. Most call-first shops end up with both halves anyway: the receptionist answers, and its follow-up text carries your booking link.
  5. Set the kill criteria now, before you're attached: one bookable call a month was the bar for trying it; the bar for keeping it is higher. In 30 days the tool has either booked jobs worth more than three months of its price, or it's gone. If your log shows fewer than ten or fifteen calls a week, give the test 60 days instead, so one slow month can't decide it. Put the review date in your calendar. (This is the Five-Question Filter from Chapter 3 doing its job.)

The takeaway: before you buy any AI to find new customers, stop losing the ones already calling you.

Chapter 5 — Marketing Without a Marketing Department

Picture the version of you that marketing advice is written for: posts three times a week, runs seasonal campaigns, "engages with the community." Now the real version: your business page last posted eleven weeks ago, the photo was good, and every day since, a small voice has said you should really post something while you did actual work instead. (That scene is an illustration, not a case study. If it stung, it's yours; if not, the loop below is still shorter than whatever you're doing now.)

Here is the honest frame for this whole chapter: marketing was never your job, and it became your job anyway. Nobody opens a shop to write captions. But the market moved word-of-mouth onto screens, so the words have to exist. And this is already the most common job owners hand the machine: in a 1,683-business Canadian survey, content creation was the top use of generative AI, named by 84% of the owners already using it (CFIB, 2025; that is 84% of the survey's 525 generative-AI users). The survey counted content creation broadly; this chapter makes the split the survey doesn't: the words one way, the pictures of what you sell another. The question was never whether owners would delegate the words. It's whether they delegate them well.

Shahezad Contractor runs Cousin's Food Inc., a Philadelphia halal restaurant group that did over $4 million in revenue in 2025. He uses an AI assistant for social content, training materials, forecasting, and site scouting, and he has explicitly avoided hiring a content writer (CNBC, March 2026; independent reporting, and a size caveat: multi-location, bigger than most readers of this book). Notice the shape of his win, because even at his bigger-than-you scale it is this book's capacity doctrine in one line: the business grew and the content got made and the marketing hire didn't happen. Not jobs cut: a hire deferred, by labor priced at assistant rates ($0–20/mo to start, the class Chapter 8 shops for; his exact stack isn't in the reporting).

And before the demo sweeps you: Victoria Savage runs Rustic Rebel Sourdough, a one-person bakery in Capreol, Ontario. She posted AI-generated teaser images of upcoming sandwiches and caught a public backlash from her own customers; she defended the choice on cost grounds, "every penny counts" (CBC Sudbury, July 2026; independent). One person, one small shop, and the customers still noticed and still minded. Hold both stories. They are the map of this domain: the words are safe to delegate, and the pictures of what you sell are a different thing entirely.

What the two questions say about marketing

Run Chapter 1's two questions over the marketing pile and something convenient appears: most of this domain is the good kind of delegation.

Can you check it at a glance? For words about your own business: yes, better than anyone alive. A draft post about this week's special, a reply to a review, a newsletter paragraph about your new hours. You know instantly whether it's true and whether it sounds like you. The check is one read.

Could you survive the worst version? Here marketing splits in two. A slightly generic caption that slips through: survivable, invisible, the cost is mild mediocrity. But marketing is public and permanent, so three specific classes fail the survivability question, and they get their own section below: images that misrepresent what you sell, claims and offers the business has to honor, and anything that trades on being authentically you when it isn't.

So the domain verdict, up front: delegate the drafting of words grounded in real events of your business, under a one-read check. Keep the camera, the claims, and the taste.

After this chapter, you can:

  • Build a one-page Voice Pack that makes every AI marketing draft sound like you
  • Run a 30-minute weekly loop that keeps your channels alive off real events
  • Name the three marketing delegations you never make, and why
  • Choose your stack ($0, ~$30–50, or content-as-strategy) on arithmetic instead of ads

The primary build: a Voice Pack and a weekly loop

This is the one build this chapter asks of you. No new paid tools required to start; the assistant you already have is enough for the loop itself.

Step 1 — the Voice Pack (30 minutes, once). One page, in your words, saved where you can paste it:

  • Who you are and who you serve, in two sentences you'd say out loud.
  • Five words you'd use; five you never would. (If "elevated" makes you gag, write it down.)
  • Your three standing facts: hours, service area, the thing you're known for.
  • Two real posts or emails you were proud of, pasted in as voice samples.
  • One rule line at the bottom: "Draft only from the facts and events I give you. If you need a fact I didn't give, ask; never invent specials, prices, or claims."

That last line is Chapter 2's no-inventing fence doing marketing duty (shape 1: the machine fills gaps with plausible inventions; a written boundary gives your check something to catch them against). The Pack is your standing instruction, because paper remembers what the worker won't.

Step 2 — the weekly loop (about 30 minutes, same day each week). Bring three real events from the week: a job finished, a product back in stock, a question three customers asked. Paste the Pack, paste the events, ask for drafts. Then the checks, in order: true? (facts against what happened) · yours? (read it aloud; would you say it across the counter?) · promises? (any price, offer, or deadline gets checked like a quote, because it is one). Fix or cut, then schedule the week. (More than one of you posting? The loop still works: "yours?" then means "matches the Pack," and whoever owns the Pack reads the week's drafts once.) Real photo where a photo is needed; the camera minutes ride along on the job itself, a phone shot while you're already on site, not a line in the marketing half-hour. Done. Worked once (labeled illustration): the event is an oven repair pushing Saturday's bread two hours late; the draft comes back "Bread's late Saturday: oven repair this morning. First loaves out at 11, croissants unaffected, day-olds half price while we catch up." True? That is what happened. Yours? It survives reading aloud. Promises? Half price is a price: recompute it, confirm you'll honor it, post. (That one goes out same-day — late bread can't wait for the schedule; the week's other drafts get scheduled as usual.)

The measured claim, in your arithmetic, not mine: count what a posted week costs you now in evenings and avoidance. The loop's price is roughly half an hour plus the checking discipline. Whether that trade clears is your number; the book promises none.

Skip Buffer if you post to one platform and can post directly: a scheduler for one channel is a subscription for a habit you don't have yet.
  • The ~$30–50/mo stack, when volume is real (prices secondary-sourced Aug 2026; vendor pages rule): Canva Pro (~$12.99/mo) + Buffer paid ($5/channel/mo) + Brevo (~$8/mo+) or Mailchimp (~$10/mo+) for email, since the list you own beats the feed you rent. The three delegations you never make here

1. Pictures of what you sell. A product photo is a promise about the physical world. AI-generated images of your food, your work, your space are invented promises, and Rustic Rebel shows customers punishing even the mildest version, teaser images of sandwiches not yet on sale, at one-person scale. The take-it-back is absolute for product imagery; decorative graphics and layout are fine, that's design, not testimony. (Selling a service with nothing to photograph? Your "product photo" is the promise-heavy claim: the turnaround time, the guarantee, the before-and-after. Same rule, no camera required.) Your phone camera plus Canva beats a beautiful lie every week. And the extreme case, a fraud story that ran on AI-made marketing (the same scam works with a rented photographer; the tools just made the lie cheap and lush): a Glasgow events company sold £35 tickets on lush AI-generated marketing for a "chocolate experience" that turned out to be a near-empty warehouse; Police were called. The organizer said about 850 ticket purchases would be refunded, a commitment rather than a verified settled count, and later reporting found some refunds still being chased. The company folded its events (BBC/STV/TIME, Feb–Mar 2024; independent). AI marketing can write checks your business can't cash. Never let it write the check.

2. Claims, offers, and prices, unchecked. "20% off through Friday" is a promise your local consumer-protection rules will likely hold you to, contract or not. Every draft containing a number, a deadline, or a guarantee gets the quote-arithmetic treatment from Chapter 1: recompute it, confirm you'll honor it, then post. And anything past a simple discount, a contest, a money-back guarantee, a claim about health, safety, or certification, gets one more stop: your state or province's rules or a professional, because arithmetic isn't the whole check there. The machine does not know your margins, and per Chapter 2, it will fill gaps with plausible inventions if you let it draft offers unsourced.

3. Being you, undisclosed, where "you" is the product. If your marketing's engine is your own voice and face (coaches, creators, personality-led shops), wholesale ghost-generation is a trust debt that compounds. Draft with the machine, sure. But anything presented as personally, authentically yours should have passed through you heavily enough that you'd defend every line across the counter. The Voice Pack makes that fast; it doesn't make it optional. That judgment call is the taste this chapter keeps yours.

A note on the popular fear behind all three: "customers hate AI content." What customers punished, in the cases this book can verify, is deception and laziness: fake product images, generic sludge, claims that don't hold. Words drafted by a machine from your real events, checked and sent in your voice, carry no such tell, because there is nothing to detect. The failure mode is not the tool. It is publishing something that was never true of you.

Do this Friday

About 45 minutes, $0.

  1. Write the Voice Pack (30 min, the five parts above). This is the whole setup cost of the domain.
  2. Run a starter loop (15 min): one real event from this week, a draft, the three checks, schedule or post it. (The full weekly loop runs three events in about 30 minutes; today is the small first rep.)
  3. Set the boundary in writing: add to the Pack: "No AI images of products or premises. No offers I haven't recomputed." Two lines, and this chapter's take-it-backs are policy instead of intentions.
  4. Calendar the loop for the same slot next week. A marketing department is mostly a calendar that doesn't negotiate.

The takeaway: delegate the words about real events of your business under a one-read check; keep the camera, the claims, and the taste, because marketing is the public, permanent end of your delegation map.

Chapter 12 — Stay Out of Trouble: The Employment Law of Machine Labor

When you hire a person, a century of employment law tells you what you owe each other. When you hire the machine, the law that matters is older and simpler, and a Canadian tribunal applied it in one small ruling: your business owns what your worker says.

Jake Moffatt booked an Air Canada flight after the airline's website chatbot explained a bereavement-fare policy: book now, claim the discount within 90 days. That version of the policy didn't exist. Air Canada's real bereavement policy required asking before the flight; the book-now-claim-later part was the bot's invention. The airline refused to honor it, and argued, in effect, that the chatbot was responsible for its own words. The British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal disagreed and made Air Canada pay: $812.02 Canadian, the fare difference plus interest and fees (Moffatt v. Air Canada, February 2024). Note the venue honestly: a provincial small-claims-style tribunal, eight hundred dollars, no grand precedent machinery. That is exactly why it belongs to you. This wasn't constitutional law; it was a customer, a screenshot, and the obvious principle that a company can't disown its own front desk, a principle much older than chatbots and alive in some form almost everywhere you could operate. The ruling binds nobody else; the principle travels. Every promise your tools make, you keep. Chapter 4 warned you when you switched the phones on; this chapter turns the warning into your shop's one-page policy.

The other legal fact of machine labor you already know from Chapter 2: the machine gives confidently wrong answers about rules. New York City's own official business-help chatbot told owners to break laws it existed to explain. So the two exposure paths are now mapped: what the machine promises outward (your liability to customers) and what the machine tells you inward (compliance advice that's wrong). One policy page covers both, plus the quiet third exposure most owners never price: what you feed in. The consumer versions of the big assistants train on conversations by default — all four below, per their own documentation as of August 2026. (These are consumer-tier defaults: business and school plans run under different rules, regions differ, and defaults move. The switch is the check that outlives the policy.) Here they are in one place:

  • ChatGPT: Settings → Data Controls.
  • Claude: Privacy Settings (opting out also cuts data retention from years to days).
  • Gemini: the "Keep Activity" setting, which defaults ON for adults.
  • Microsoft Copilot (the consumer version you'd sign into at home; work and school accounts are excluded): its privacy settings, a default Microsoft announced in August 2024, with the opt-out controls following that October.

Menus move (vendor documentation as of Aug 2026, so confirm the path the day you flip it), but the principle doesn't: untrained accounts are the only accounts a business should use, and the toggle is a tonight job, not a someday job.

The primary build: one page, three rules

Long governance documents are for companies with lawyers on payroll. Yours is one page on the wall, three rules, dated and signed by everyone who touches a work account, you first, and by every new hire before their first login to a work account: day one, not quarter-end. The three rules answer the three exposures in the order you can control them: what goes in, what goes out, what you believe. Here it is, ready to adapt:

RULE 1 — WHAT GOES IN. Nothing we'd have to apologize for losing. No customer names tied to their business, no payment or banking details, no health information, no employee records (pay, home addresses, medical or disciplinary details), nothing under an NDA, into any consumer AI tool, ever. Training opt-outs set on every account before first use (the four switches above); accounts are work accounts, not personal ones. Recordings and transcripts of other people require telling them first and getting a yes where the law asks for one (Chapter 7), and any tool that stores them gets its data page read before the first meeting: storage, sharing, retention, deletion, and whether your exact plan trains its models. The test for gray areas: would you pin it to the shop door? Then it doesn't go in the chat either.

RULE 2 — WHAT GOES OUT. Nothing sends itself. Anything AI-drafted that reaches a customer gets one human read first (Chapter 1's oldest check). Anything with a number, date, price, or commitment gets recomputed by a person (Chapter 2, standing). Customer-facing bots run on a narrow script: the questions we wrote, the bookings we allow, and "I'll have someone confirm that" for everything else. No improvised policies, prices, or guarantees, because of the airline story on the back of this page. If a tool makes a promise anyway: small ones we keep once, like Air Canada should have kept its CA$812 one. Bigger ones: the page names the largest make-good we'd honor without a meeting, and anything above it gets a person on the phone, fast and honest, before anything is honored. Either way we fix the script the same day: the error survived its check, so the task comes back (Chapter 1's firing rule, now company policy).

RULE 3 — WHAT WE BELIEVE. Rules come from the source of the rule. No legal, tax, safety, or compliance question gets settled by a chatbot, ours or anyone's; the NYC story is what official-looking wrongness looks like. The machine may brief the professional (draft our questions, organize our documents); the professional or the primary source decides. And no AI checks another AI's work and calls it verified: a Texas lawyer tried exactly that, the second tool missed the fabrications, and the court fined him $2,000 with mandatory AI education on top (Chapter 2 carries the case and its record). Checks run against reality: our documents, our numbers, the actual regulation, a qualified human.

That's the page. Two footers make it real. Disclosure: our bot says it's an AI up front, because some places require that by law for bots that book or sell; if a customer asks anyway, we say yes, cheerfully; and the human path is always staffed (Chapter 4's handoff warning, now policy). Review: the page gets re-read at the quarterly audit (Chapter 13) and the four training switches get re-checked with it, because tools and menus change, and the policy that nobody re-reads is a poster, not a policy.

The measured claim, computable as always, and recomputed the way Rule 2 demands: ten minutes of switches, 45 minutes of writing, the fifteen-minute talk, five for the bot check — 75 minutes end to end at the full budget, often closer to 65, and if the talk happens on the clock, your crew's time is part of the price too. (Budget the full fifteen; a six-person counter walkthrough often finishes in five, and solo means a talk with yourself. Whatever it actually takes is the number that goes on your page.) Price all of it against one Moffatt-sized make-good (CA$812, in the real case), one lost client whose data you'd have to confess to pasting, or one afternoon untangling advice a bot invented. And those are the cheap failures: a leaked health or payment record can carry breach-notification duties and statutory exposure, a number with more digits than any make-good. Under two hours, once, against any of those: the downside case alone carries this chapter's argument.

The take-backs, named

  1. "The AI said it was legal." Not a sentence that exists. There is no defense, no refund, and no professional standard behind it; Rule 3 exists so it's never uttered in your shop.
  2. Compliance-by-chatbot. Minimum wage, tips, scheduling laws, tax thresholds: source-of-rule or professional, every time. Free answers about laws are priced exactly right.
  3. The unattended bot with a wide script. Every capability you add to a customer-facing bot is surface area on Rule 2. Keep the script narrow, keep a person ready to step in, and sample its answers weekly (Chapter 9's ritual). A bot you can't audit this week is a bot you shouldn't run this week.
  4. Personal accounts for work. The cheapest data leak there is: work pasted into an account whose settings, retention, and training status you've never seen. Work accounts, opt-outs set, or it isn't work.

Do this Friday

About 75 minutes of your time, $0 in tools. (Step 3 happens on paid time if you have staff; that's real money, and it still clears.)

  1. Tonight, the switches (10 min for the accounts you hold): every assistant account you personally hold gets its training opt-out set. Screenshot each settings page; the screenshots are your policy's appendix and your proof-of-diligence habit starting now. Staff on personal accounts is its own job: this week, ask what each person actually uses and move the work onto shop-controlled accounts, priced in days, not in tonight's ten minutes. Then scroll your oldest chats once (call it 15 minutes, its own box): whatever got pasted last year is still sitting there. Delete what you can, and if what you find is a customer's card number or health detail, that is a Rule 3 call: a privacy professional this week, not at the quarterly.
  2. Write the page (45 min): adapt the three rules above in your own words, your examples. Keep it to one page even if it hurts; length is where policies go to die.
  3. The talk (budget 15 min, schedule it today): walk the page, everyone signs, it goes on the wall and in the drive. Six staff at the counter often finishes in five, not a conference; solo means you sign it and mean it; a dispersed or shift crew means catching each person once this week, a few minutes each, with the time scaling by headcount, not by this page. Pair it with Chapter 11's speech if you haven't given it (those minutes belong to Chapter 11's own Friday, not this page's 75); they're the same conversation about the same honesty.
  4. The bot audit (5 min, if you run one): read your customer-facing bot's script against Rule 2. Anything it could promise that you wouldn't honor gets cut today, not at the quarterly.

The takeaway: your business owns what its machines leak, say, and believe — so run one page and three rules: nothing confidential goes in, nothing unread goes out, and rules come from the source of the rule, never the bot.
